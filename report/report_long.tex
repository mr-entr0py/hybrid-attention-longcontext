\begin{center}{\large\bfseries Abstract}\end{center}
\vspace{-0.15cm}
\begin{list}{}{\setlength{\leftmargin}{0.5in}\setlength{\rightmargin}{0.5in}}\item[]
Long context has become a defining capability of large language models, and in particular of agents
that must carry an entire working history through many steps. The cost of that capability is set by
the attention mechanism: full softmax attention keeps a key--value cache that grows with every token,
whereas linear and recurrent alternatives compress the past into a state of fixed size.
Most recent long-context architectures are therefore hybrids that interleave a few full-attention
layers with many linear ones, at ratios such as 3:1 or 7:1. We find that these ratios are generally
inherited from earlier models rather than derived from long-context evidence, and that no published
study isolates how the \emph{amount} of full attention governs long-context behaviour as a function of
task type and input length. This project studies that question directly. We train a family of
12-layer language models from scratch in which the only change between models is the number $k$ of
token-mixing layers that use full attention; the remaining $12-k$ layers use Gated DeltaNet.
Parameters, data, data order and the training recipe are held fixed. The models will be evaluated
on retrieval, multi-hop and aggregation tasks at 1K--32K tokens, all inside the training length,
and their inference memory and latency will be measured on the same implementation. The deliverable
is a capability--cost frontier with pre-registered tests of whether a small number of full-attention
layers recovers most of the long-context ability of an all-attention model. This report describes the
motivation, the theory, the design and the engineering and validation work completed so far. That
work includes a verified training and evaluation stack and the reproduction of published reference
results on a single 16\,GB RTX~5080. No main-study model has been trained yet, so the report makes
no claims about the hypotheses.
\end{list}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Language models are increasingly asked to read and act on very long inputs: whole codebases,
book-length documents, multi-hour transcripts and, most demandingly, the accumulated history of an
agent that plans, calls tools and observes their results over many steps. For such systems the
context window is working memory. What the model can recover from early in its context bounds what
it can reason about later, and the cost of keeping that context available bounds how long it can run
and how many users it can serve at once.

Both properties are determined largely by the token-mixing layer. In a Transformer
\citep{vaswani2017attention} every layer attends over all previous tokens, so it can in principle
retrieve any earlier token exactly. The price is a key--value (KV) cache that grows linearly with the
context and a prefill cost that grows quadratically. Linear-attention and state-space layers
\citep{katharopoulos2020transformers,gu2023mamba,dao2024transformers,yang2025gated} reverse the trade:
they summarise the past in a recurrent state of fixed size, so memory and per-token decoding cost
do not depend on the context length. But a fixed-size state is a lossy memory, and a growing body of
theory and experiment shows that such models struggle with precisely the operations long context is
meant to enable, namely exact recall and copying from far back in the input
\citep{arora2023zoology,jelassi2024repeat,wen2024rnns}.

Hybrid architectures attempt to get the best of both. Jamba \citep{lieber2024jamba},
Griffin \citep{de2024griffin}, Nemotron-H \citep{nvidia2025nemotronh}, MiniMax-01
\citep{minimax2025minimax01}, Qwen3-Next \citep{qwen2025qwen3next} and Kimi Linear
\citep{kimi2025linear} all keep a small number of full-attention layers and make the rest linear or
recurrent, typically in a fixed ratio such as one attention layer in four or one in eight.
The design question these models answer implicitly is \emph{how much full attention is enough}.
When we traced where their ratios come from, we found that the answer is usually inherited. Nemotron-H,
for example, adopts its attention fraction from an earlier NVIDIA study rather than testing it, and
neither MiniMax-01 nor Qwen3-Next publishes the sweep behind its choice (\cref{sec:related}). The
controlled sweeps that do exist mostly measure language-modelling loss, which turns out to be nearly
insensitive to the ratio once a few attention layers are present, while the one sweep that reports
long-context recall averages it over tasks and lengths.

This project asks the question directly and under controlled conditions:
\emph{as the number of full-attention layers in a hybrid model is varied, with everything else held
fixed, how do retrieval, multi-hop reasoning and aggregation over long inputs change, and what does
each additional full-attention layer cost at inference time?} The aim is a capability--cost
frontier rather than a single recommended ratio. The frontier should tell us whether a small amount
of full attention recovers most of what full attention provides, whether the answer depends on the
kind of long-context operation, and how it changes with input length.

\paragraph{Why small models trained from scratch.}
The question concerns language models in general, and its practical stakes lie with large models.
Answering it causally, however, requires many models that differ in exactly one architectural
factor and are otherwise identical: same parameter count, same data in the same order, same
optimiser and schedule, with several random seeds each. Retrofitting or distilling existing large
checkpoints cannot provide this, because the converted model inherits representations learned under
a different architecture. We therefore train models of about 28M--49M parameters from scratch on a
single NVIDIA RTX~5080 with 16\,GB of memory. Scale is the price we pay for control, and every
conclusion will be scoped accordingly. The mechanisms under study, however, are scale-independent in
form: KV-cache growth, fixed-state interference and the attention/recurrence trade-off. Controlled
small-scale evidence is how such mechanisms have repeatedly been characterised first
\citep{arora2023zoology,waleffe2024empirical,wang2025systematic}.

\paragraph{Contribution and current status.}
The intended contribution is a controlled, seed-replicated, length- and task-resolved measurement of
the composition effect, together with inference costs measured on one implementation. This is an
extension and partial replication of existing ratio studies, not the first of its kind
(\cref{sec:related}). At the time of writing the study is at its pre-pilot stage. The literature
review, the experimental protocol and its independent review, the full training and evaluation
infrastructure, a battery of verification tests and the reproduction of published reference results
are complete. The main training runs have not started. \Cref{sec:validation} reports the completed
engineering work and \cref{sec:plan} the remaining plan.\enlargethispage{2\baselineskip}

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}

Throughout, a sequence of $T$ tokens is represented in each layer by vectors
$\vect{x}_1,\dots,\vect{x}_T\in\R^{d}$. Each layer has a \emph{token mixer}, which moves information
between positions, followed by a position-wise feed-forward network. The architectures discussed
here differ only in the mixer.

\subsection{Full softmax attention and the KV cache}
A causal attention head with dimension $d_h$ projects each token to a query, key and value,
$\vect{q}_t=W_Q\vect{x}_t$, $\vect{k}_t=W_K\vect{x}_t$, $\vect{v}_t=W_V\vect{x}_t$, and outputs
\begin{equation}\label{eq:attn}
  \vect{o}_t \;=\; \sum_{i\le t}
  \frac{\exp\!\big(\vect{q}_t^{\T}\vect{k}_i/\sqrt{d_h}\big)}
       {\sum_{j\le t}\exp\!\big(\vect{q}_t^{\T}\vect{k}_j/\sqrt{d_h}\big)}\;\vect{v}_i .
\end{equation}
Every output is a convex combination of \emph{all} earlier values, with weights chosen by
content. This is what gives attention its retrieval ability: a query that matches one key much more
strongly than the others returns that key's value almost exactly, however far back it occurred and
however many other tokens intervene.

The cost follows from the same structure. During training or prefill over $T$ tokens, the score
matrix has $T^2/2$ entries per head, so time grows as $O(T^2 d)$. Memory-efficient kernels such as
FlashAttention \citep{dao2022flashattention,dao2023flashattention2} avoid materialising this matrix,
but they do not change the arithmetic. During autoregressive decoding, \cref{eq:attn} requires every
past key and value, which are kept in the KV cache. For a layer of width $d$ stored in 16-bit
precision the cache occupies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kv}
  M_{\Full}(T) \;=\; 2\cdot T\cdot d\cdot 2\ \text{bytes} \;=\; 4\,T d\ \text{bytes},
\end{equation}
and each decoding step must read all of it. At long context, decoding is therefore limited by memory
bandwidth and the number of concurrent sequences is limited by memory capacity, both in proportion
to $T$. In our D384 model, for instance, one full-attention layer holds 1.5\,KB per token, or 48\,MiB
for a single 32K-token sequence.

\subsection{Linear attention as a recurrent associative memory}
Linear attention replaces the exponential kernel in \cref{eq:attn} by a dot product of feature maps,
$\exp(\vect{q}^{\T}\vect{k})\approx\phi(\vect{q})^{\T}\phi(\vect{k})$
\citep{katharopoulos2020transformers}. Re-associating the sum then turns attention into a recurrence
on a matrix-valued state $S_t\in\R^{d_v\times d_k}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:linattn}
  S_t \;=\; S_{t-1} + \vect{v}_t\,\phi(\vect{k}_t)^{\T},
  \qquad
  \vect{o}_t \;=\; S_t\,\phi(\vect{q}_t)
\end{equation}
(up to a normaliser that modern variants usually drop). The state has the same size at every step,
so decoding costs $O(d_k d_v)$ per head per token and memory is independent of $T$. Training remains
parallel through chunkwise algorithms that combine intra-chunk attention with inter-chunk recurrence
\citep{yang2024gla}.

\Cref{eq:linattn} is best read as an associative memory. Each token writes the outer product of
its value and key into $S$, and a query reads $S\vect{q}=\sum_i\vect{v}_i(\vect{k}_i^{\T}\vect{q})$.
Retrieval is exact only if the stored keys are orthogonal to the query's target. A $d_k$-dimensional
key space holds at most $d_k$ mutually orthogonal keys, so once more associations are written they
interfere, and the more of them a sequence contains, the noisier every read becomes
\citep{schlag2021linear}. Much of the recent progress on linear attention can be understood as
better strategies for managing this finite memory. These include forgetting through data-dependent
decay \citep{yang2024gla,dao2024transformers} and overwriting rather than merely accumulating
\citep{schlag2021linear,yang2024deltanet}.

\subsection{The delta rule and Gated DeltaNet}\label{sec:gdn}
The delta rule treats each step as an online regression that makes the state map the current key to
the current value \citep{schlag2021linear}. A gradient step of size $\beta_t\in(0,1)$ on the
loss $\tfrac12\lVert S\vect{k}_t-\vect{v}_t\rVert^2$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:delta}
  S_t \;=\; S_{t-1} - \beta_t\big(S_{t-1}\vect{k}_t-\vect{v}_t\big)\vect{k}_t^{\T}
      \;=\; S_{t-1}\big(I-\beta_t\vect{k}_t\vect{k}_t^{\T}\big) + \beta_t\vect{v}_t\vect{k}_t^{\T}.
\end{equation}
Unlike \cref{eq:linattn}, which only adds, the delta rule first removes the value currently associated
with $\vect{k}_t$ and then writes the new one, so a key that is updated is overwritten rather than
blurred. DeltaNet \citep{yang2024deltanet} made this rule efficient to train in parallel, and
Gated DeltaNet \citep{yang2025gated} adds a data-dependent decay $\alpha_t\in(0,1)$ that can clear
the memory when the context changes:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gdn}
  S_t \;=\; S_{t-1}\,\alpha_t\big(I-\beta_t\vect{k}_t\vect{k}_t^{\T}\big) + \beta_t\vect{v}_t\vect{k}_t^{\T},
  \qquad \vect{o}_t = S_t\vect{q}_t .
\end{equation}
Here $\vect{q}_t$ and $\vect{k}_t$ are L2-normalised, and $\alpha_t$ and $\beta_t$ are produced from
$\vect{x}_t$ by small learned projections. A short causal convolution precedes the projections, and
the output passes through a normalisation and a learned gate. The decay lets the layer forget
selectively, and the delta update lets it edit specific associations. In published comparisons this
combination outperforms both Mamba-2 and DeltaNet on recall-intensive tasks at equal size
\citep{yang2025gated}. It is also the linear layer of Qwen3-Next. We adopt it as our linear mixer for
these reasons, and because it has mature Triton kernels \citep{fla2024} that run on our GPU
(\cref{sec:env}).

\subsection{Hybrid models and the cost of each full-attention layer}\label{sec:hybridcost}
A hybrid model assigns to each layer $l$ one of the two mixers. Let $k$ of its $L$ layers use full
attention. Using \cref{eq:kv} and the fixed size $S_{\Lin}$ of a linear layer's state, the inference
state of one sequence of length $T$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:state}
  M(k,T) \;=\; k\cdot 4Td \;+\; (L-k)\cdot S_{\Lin}\quad\text{bytes},
\end{equation}
which grows linearly in $T$ with slope proportional to $k$, and is constant in $T$ only when $k=0$.
In our D384 configuration each Gated DeltaNet layer stores 3 heads of $64\times128$ fp32 values plus
a convolution cache, $S_{\Lin}=110{,}592$ bytes, while a full-attention layer stores 1,536 bytes per
token. A single full-attention layer therefore overtakes a linear layer's entire memory after only
72 tokens, and at 32K tokens the twelve-layer model needs 1.3\,MiB with $k=0$ but 576\,MiB with
$k=12$. Prefill compute behaves similarly. Each full-attention layer adds a term quadratic in $T$,
while the linear layers stay linear. Decoding latency adds a per-step read of the KV cache that
grows with $T$.

These are the costs a hybrid saves. The open question is what it gives up. Because the linear layers'
state is fixed, a hybrid's ability to retrieve something from far back depends on whether the few
full-attention layers can do the retrieving, and whether the surrounding linear layers deliver
representations they can use. That depends on the number and placement of those layers, on the kind
of long-context operation, and potentially on the length of the input. These are exactly the
variables this study manipulates.

\section{What is known, and what is not}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Production hybrids.}
The models that define current practice report their ratios but rarely their reasons. Jamba
\citep{lieber2024jamba} compared two ratios (1:3 and 1:7 attention to Mamba) and found virtually no
difference in language-modelling metrics. Nemotron-H \citep{nvidia2025nemotronh} sets its attention layers to roughly 8\% of the network
(4 of 52 in the 8B model) ``as suggested by prior work'', namely the study of
\citet{waleffe2024empirical}, rather than by a new ablation. Its comparison against a Transformer trained on the same data also changes depth and
positional encoding, so it is not a controlled ratio comparison. MiniMax-01 \citep{minimax2025minimax01}
(one softmax layer in eight) and Qwen3-Next \citep{qwen2025qwen3next} (one in four) publish no sweep
behind their choices. Kimi Linear's ablation \citep{kimi2025linear} varies the ratio but reports
perplexity only.

\paragraph{Controlled sweeps measure loss, and loss is nearly flat.}
Several studies vary the ratio with model size fixed
\citep{waleffe2024empirical,dao2024transformers,bae2025hybrid,wang2025systematic,kimi2025linear}.
Their loss results agree closely. Once roughly 5--10\% of layers use attention, validation loss
is flat or U-shaped in the ratio, the first attention layer captures most of the gain, and an
all-attention model is often worse than the best hybrid (\cref{fig:prior}a).

\paragraph{Recall is not flat.}
The only controlled sweep we found that reports a long-context recall metric is that of
\citet{wang2025systematic}. In their study, recall averaged over four linear mechanisms rises from
0.256 for purely linear models to 0.397 with one attention layer in four, while language-modelling
metrics barely move (\cref{fig:prior}b). The Gated DeltaNet hybrids start higher, because the delta
rule is itself a comparatively good recall mechanism, and they improve markedly only once attention
reaches one layer in seven. This dissociation between loss and recall is the empirical starting
point of our study. It is consistent with the theory of fixed-state models: recall from a fixed
state degrades with the number of stored associations \citep{arora2023zoology,arora2024simple},
copying requires state that grows with the copied length \citep{jelassi2024repeat}, and recurrent
models need attention-like components to solve associative retrieval efficiently
\citep{wen2024rnns}. Wang et al. report recall only as an average over RULER tasks
\citep{hsieh2024ruler} at a single context length, and found recall at 340M parameters too weak to
report. That already signals the measurement problem a small-model study must solve
(\cref{sec:eval}).

\paragraph{Length and task are almost untested.}
We found only one experiment that crosses the amount of full attention with input length.
In Nemotron-Flash \citep{fu2025nemotronflash}, keeping one full-attention layer instead of three in a
3B model leaves short-context recall unchanged but lowers needle retrieval at 32K tokens from 0.69 to
0.32. That experiment uses sliding-window rather than linear layers and continued pre-training
rather than training from scratch. Other long-context comparisons confound the architecture with
positional extrapolation beyond the training length. Evidence that different long-context operations
need different amounts of attention is indirect, coming from per-task tables of single hybrid models
and from retrofitting studies. Placement has been studied from scratch only on loss, where its effect
is negligible \citep{bae2025hybrid}.

\paragraph{Confounds that a controlled study must handle.}
The literature also identifies variables that can masquerade as composition effects. Positional
encoding is the most important: removing rotary embeddings \citep{su2024roformer} from the attention
layers of hybrids can improve long-context retrieval substantially \citep{qiao2026nope}, and several
production hybrids do so. Small all-attention models are sometimes handicapped relative to hybrids,
so the reference model itself must be checked for competence. The implementation also matters:
FlashAttention reaches a much larger fraction of the hardware's peak than current linear-attention
kernels, so measured latencies compare kernel maturity as well as algorithms.

\paragraph{The gap.}
No study we located combines a full sweep from all-linear to all-attention under matched parameters,
data and recipe, with replicate seeds; results separated by task family and resolved by length, with
every length inside the training range; placement trained from scratch and measured on retrieval;
and inference cost measured on a single implementation. Our study is designed to supply this
combination at small scale. We expect to \emph{reproduce} two known regularities, flat loss and
saturating recall. What is new is the controlled, length- and task-resolved shape of the recall
curve and its relation to measured cost.

\section{Research questions and hypotheses}\label{sec:rq}

Let each model have $L=12$ layers, and let the architecture vector
$\vect{a}=(a_0,\dots,a_{11})\in\{\Full,\Lin\}^{12}$ record which layers use full attention ($\Full$)
and which use Gated DeltaNet ($\Lin$). The composition is $k=\sum_l\mathbb{1}[a_l=\Full]$, with
full-attention fraction $r=k/12$. For a task family $f$ and evaluation length $T$, let
$S_f(k,T)$ be the per-seed accuracy. We summarise how much of the all-attention advantage a hybrid
recovers by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:recovery}
  G_f(T) = S_f(12,T)-S_f(0,T),\qquad
  R_f(k,T) = \frac{S_f(k,T)-S_f(0,T)}{G_f(T)},
\end{equation}
so that $R=0$ means no better than the all-linear model and $R=1$ means as good as the all-attention
one. The study is organised around four questions:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2em,style=unboxed,itemsep=2pt,font=\normalfont\bfseries]
\item[RQ-A Composition.] How does long-context performance depend on $k$ when everything else is held fixed?
\item[RQ-B Placement.] At a fixed $k$, does it matter whether the full-attention layers are early, central or late?
\item[RQ-C Task dependence.] Do retrieval, multi-hop composition and aggregation need different amounts of full attention?
\item[RQ-D Cost.] How do capability differences relate to inference memory, prefill latency and decoding throughput?
\end{description}

\Cref{tab:hyp} lists the hypotheses. They were written down, together with their tests and
falsification rules, before any model was trained. They are hypotheses to be tested, not
expectations to be confirmed. The central one, H1, is operationalised as a recovery of at least
80\%. Equivalently, the contrast $L_3=S(3)-0.2\,S(0)-0.8\,S(12)$ is positive. It is tested at the
longest evaluation lengths, 16K and 32K, on retrieval.

We also recorded predictions in advance, which are not used in any test. They are that one
full-attention layer recovers little retrieval, that three recover between 60\% and 110\%, that
multi-hop recovers less than retrieval, and that the gap between the extremes widens with length.
Writing them down protects the analysis from post-hoc narratives in either direction.

\section{Study design}\label{sec:design}

\subsection{The model family}\label{sec:models}
Every model is a stack of 12 pre-norm blocks with tied input and output embeddings and no bias terms
(\cref{fig:arch}). Each block applies RMSNorm \citep{zhang2019rmsnorm}, the layer's mixer and a
residual connection, then RMSNorm, a SwiGLU feed-forward network \citep{shazeer2020glu} and a second
residual connection. Only the mixer differs between layers, and only the mixer differs between
models.

The full-attention mixer $\Full$ is multi-head attention with 64-dimensional heads, per-head
normalisation of queries and keys \citep{henry2020qknorm}, rotary position embeddings with base $5\times10^5$, the value
used by Llama~3 for contexts up to 32K tokens \citep{dubey2024llama3}, and a head-wise sigmoid output
gate \citep{qiu2025gated}. Positions restart at each document boundary. The linear mixer $\Lin$ is
Gated DeltaNet (\cref{eq:gdn}) as implemented in the \code{fla} library (version~0.6.0;
\citealp{fla2024}), with 64-dimensional keys, 128-dimensional values, a width-4 short convolution and
an output gate. The full-attention layers are spaced evenly, at positions
$p_j=\lfloor(j+\tfrac12)\cdot12/k\rfloor$ for $j=0,\dots,k-1$.

Three controls address the confounds of \cref{sec:related}. Gated DeltaNet has a short convolution
that standard attention lacks, and such convolutions are known to help attention form induction
circuits \citep{olsson2022induction}. A control therefore adds the same convolution to the attention
layers. A second control removes positional encoding from the attention layers
\citep{kazemnejad2023nope}. A third replaces Gated DeltaNet by a variant with a four-times larger
per-head state, which asks whether a larger recurrent state can substitute for a KV cache at a
fraction of its memory.

\subsection{Data and training}\label{sec:training}
Training data are FineWeb-Edu \citep{penedo2024fineweb} and the PG19 corpus of books
\citep{rae2020compressive}, both pinned to exact dataset revisions. The books are needed because
web documents are short: in our tokenisation only about 2\% of FineWeb-Edu tokens lie in documents of
32K tokens or more, against 97\% for PG19. We trained our own 16,384-entry byte-level BPE tokenizer
with each digit as a separate token, so that numeric answers have a fixed, unambiguous tokenisation.
A small vocabulary also keeps the embedding from dominating the parameter budget. Documents are
packed with separators, and both mixers are masked so that no information flows across document
boundaries.

Every configuration follows the same three-phase recipe (\cref{fig:pipeline}). Stage~A trains at 4K
tokens on 0.6B (D384) or 0.8B (D512) tokens, mostly web text with 15\% book windows. Stage~B extends
to 32K tokens with a book-heavy mixture and a decaying learning rate, so that every evaluation length
lies inside the training length and no comparison depends on extrapolation. A short task-exposure
fine-tune (TEF) follows, identical for every configuration, on synthetic long-context tasks drawn from
disjoint vocabularies and templates. It is needed because models of this size cannot express
long-context retrieval zero-shot through prompts. The fine-tune elicits the ability, and the frozen
evaluation measures how far each architecture can realise it. Zero-shot measurements taken before
the fine-tune are reported alongside, so that elicited and spontaneous ability are not conflated.
All runs use AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019adamw} with $\beta=(0.9,0.95)$, weight decay 0.1, gradient
clipping at 1.0, a z-loss and bf16 autocast. The learning rate is chosen by a small sweep at
$k\in\{0,3,12\}$ scored on held-out language-modelling loss, never on task metrics.

\subsection{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}
Standard long-context benchmarks are unsuitable at this scale. Natural-language suites such as
LongBench or RULER's instruction-style prompts \citep{hsieh2024ruler} score at the floor for models
of a few tens of millions of parameters, so they could not discriminate between architectures. We
therefore use cloze-style synthetic tasks, scored by teacher-forced exact match of a short answer.
Each task controls exactly what must be retrieved and from where (\cref{fig:items}). Every item is
constructed in token space to be exactly $T$ tokens long, with filler drawn from held-out documents,
and the item files are frozen and hashed before evaluation.

The retrieval family varies the number of distractors and, in R-load, the number of stored
associations. It thereby probes the capacity limit of a fixed state directly (\cref{sec:gdn}).
Multi-hop chains of up to four links test whether retrieved information can be composed.
Aggregation tests whether information spread over the whole context can be combined. Each
confirmatory cell has 200--300 contexts with four queries each, enough that the
item-level standard error is small compared with variation between training seeds. A diagnostic
suite separates distance from distractor load, measures overwriting and recency, places the
relevant facts immediately before the query to test long input without long distance, and records
attention mass on the needles. Gold-answer log-probability and candidate-rank accuracy are kept as
secondary metrics because they degrade more gracefully than exact match.

\subsection{Cost measurement}
Inference cost is reported in two complementary forms. The primary measures are analytic: exact
state bytes from \cref{eq:state}, FLOPs per token, bytes moved per decoding step and a roofline
latency bound. These do not depend on kernel quality. The secondary measures are measured: prefill
latency, per-token decoding latency and the largest batch that fits a fixed memory budget, at 1K to
32K tokens. They are taken with exact-shape CUDA graphs, pinned attention back-ends, cold and warm
caches, at least 30 timed replays reported as the minimum, and only while no other process is using
the GPU. Measured latencies are explicitly scoped to ``this implementation on this GPU'', because
the attention and linear-attention kernels differ in maturity.

\subsection{Statistical plan and integrity rules}\label{sec:stats}
The unit of inference is the training run. Each configuration is trained with four to five seeds,
and seeds are paired across configurations: the same seed index gives the same data order and the
same initialisation of all shared modules. Paired per-seed contrasts therefore remove most
seed-to-seed variation. H1 is tested by a one-sided paired $t$-test on $L_3$, inside a Holm-corrected
family with H1$_{\min}$, H3 and H5. A hierarchical paired bootstrap over seeds and items and a
pooled-variance model serve as sensitivity analyses; if either disagrees in sign, the verdict is
downgraded to ``suggestive''. Seeds may be added only by a rule that looks at residual variance,
never at the effect. ``Falsified'' requires that the reference model be demonstrably competent and
that the verdict survive both a longer fine-tune and a doubled Stage~B, so that an under-trained
model cannot produce a false negative.

The project's working rules follow from the same concern. Every threshold, test and gate is
written down and hashed before the experiment it governs. A criterion is never restated after its
test has run. A failed check is recorded as \emph{not passed as registered}, together with either
a named residual risk or a new pre-registered check. Every design choice is logged with its
evidence, the alternatives rejected, and the observation that would reopen it.
\Cref{tab:rivals} lists the most consequential alternative explanations and the experiment that
distinguishes each from the hypothesis.

\paragraph{How the study is being run.}
In keeping with the course theme, the research process is itself agent-assisted. LLM-based agents
carry out delegated roles under a written protocol. Specialist literature agents read and annotated
the primary sources. Two independent adversarial reviewers critiqued the initial proposal, each wrote
a competing design, and they then debated the differences; this process resolved 29 issues before
the protocol was frozen. Separate builder agents wrote each software module, and independent verifier
agents, who never review their own code, tested it and returned defects for repair. The repository is
the shared memory: every decision, review, pre-registration and result is a versioned file.
The arrangement made it practical to apply checks that a small team could not otherwise afford,
such as three independent scorers for one benchmark. It also made role separation and
pre-registration essential, because a confident but wrong agent is the characteristic failure of
such systems.

\section{Engineering, validation and preliminary measurements}\label{sec:validation}

This section reports completed work. None of it bears on the hypotheses. It establishes that the
instrument is sound before it is used.

\subsection{Compute environment and constraints}\label{sec:env}
All work runs on a single workstation with one NVIDIA GeForce RTX~5080 (Blackwell, compute capability
12.0, 16\,GB GDDR7), 24 CPU cores and 62\,GB of RAM, under Ubuntu~24.04. The software stack is
PyTorch~2.11 (CUDA~12.8 build), Triton~3.6, \code{flash-linear-attention}~0.6.0 and
\code{transformers}~5.22, with exact versions locked in the repository. Being a new architecture,
sm\_120 constrained the design. Gated DeltaNet's Triton kernels run on it, forward and backward,
including variable-length packing. The official Mamba kernels and external FlashAttention wheels do
not exist for this PyTorch version, which is one reason a Mamba arm is out of scope. Attention uses
PyTorch's built-in variable-length FlashAttention-2 path.

Three further constraints shape the plan. First, memory: each process is capped at about 7\,GiB so
that experiments can coexist with other users of the machine. A 32K-token training step therefore
uses activation checkpointing; the measured peak is 6.0\,GiB. Second, the GPU also drives the
workstation's display, whose driver watchdog terminates long kernels. Seven such faults (Xid~8) were
recorded during early reproduction runs. Profiling showed that our longest 32K-token kernel takes
25\,ms, far below the watchdog limit, so the cause is still being localised by a pre-registered soak
test before any expensive run. Third, timing measurements require exclusive use of the GPU, which
is available only in scheduled windows. The essential tier of the study is budgeted at roughly 86
GPU-hours at the D384 scale and 144 at D512.

\subsection{Infrastructure verification}
The training and evaluation stack (models, deterministic data pipeline, item generators, scorer,
trainer and pre-registered analysis) was checked against 20 families of verification tests with
numeric thresholds fixed in advance. All 447 tests in these families pass. Three checks carry the
most weight. When the weights of our all-attention model are transplanted into the reference
\code{Qwen3ForCausalLM} implementation, the logits agree to a relative error of $7\times10^{-7}$.
The same check for our all-Gated-DeltaNet model against the reference \code{fla} model gives
$4.6\times10^{-4}$, within the $10^{-3}$ bound. Inference state measured on the device equals
\cref{eq:state} to the byte for every configuration. Cached decoding reproduces a full forward pass,
causal masking and document isolation hold in both mixers, and batches are a pure function of seed
and step, so interrupted runs resume exactly.

Independent verification paid for itself. The verifiers found that the no-filler control for the
multi-hop task had been generated unshuffled, which made it trivially solvable and would have biased
the comparison towards linear layers. They found that replay data in the task-exposure stage let
attention and recurrent state cross document boundaries, and that a pre-registered rule had not been
implemented. They also found that about 12.7K held-out documents duplicated training text; these were
removed. Four tests passed only under an interpretation of their thresholds that was made after the
fact. Following the integrity rule, the gate was therefore recorded as \emph{not passed as
registered}, with each residual risk named, rather than relabelled as a pass.

The most informative reproduction is multi-query associative recall

where Gated DeltaNet's state is small enough that recall falls to 0.30 on the hardest configuration,

threshold of 0.115. The phenomenon our study builds on is therefore present, measurable and
faithfully implemented in our stack.

Gated DeltaNet checkpoint of \citet{wang2025systematic} our scorer reproduces the published accuracy,
which also confirms that the \code{fla} version we use preserves the semantics the model was trained
with. The Pythia anchors failed as registered, and the failure was instructive. Our scorer,
\code{lm-evaluation-harness} and a third scorer written independently by an auditor agree item for
item across 10,306 items. The discrepancy arises because the published numbers were produced with
half-precision weights, and with fp16 weights we reproduce them to within 0.0014. The scorer is
correct, and the registered tolerance had assumed that precision would not matter. The exactness
tests behaved similarly. Three failures came from tolerances that had been fixed without
calibration, and an independent audit confirmed that each lies at the level of floating-point
rounding, for example one or two units in the last place of fp32 gradients of magnitude $10^3$.
The fourth exposed a real defect: in 7 of 39,800 queries, two facts' values shared a prefix. The
generator has been fixed, and its re-check is pre-registered.

There the multilayer perceptrons and projections dominate, and the attention kernels are more
efficient than the recurrent ones. Its quadratic term becomes visible at 32K. The resulting budget is
modest: a complete D384 run of all three phases takes roughly two GPU-hours.

The first of two planned inference-cost sessions (\cref{fig:cost}) covered nine configurations at
both scales, with 1,678 timed series. At 32K tokens the all-linear D384 model holds 1.3\,MiB of state
per sequence and so fits more than five thousand concurrent sequences in 8\,GiB. With three
full-attention layers the state is 145\,MiB and 56 sequences fit; with twelve it is 576\,MiB and only
14 fit. Prefill at 32K takes 52\,ms, 77\,ms and 154\,ms respectively. Decoding latency for the
all-linear model is flat at 0.87\,ms per token from 1K to 32K tokens, whereas with twelve attention
layers it rises from 1.17 to 1.84\,ms. These numbers show concretely what is being traded. The
study's purpose is to measure what each step along this cost curve buys in long-context capability.

\section{Plan, risks and timeline}\label{sec:plan}

The study proceeds through stages separated by quantitative go/no-go gates, each fixed before the
stage it governs (\cref{fig:roadmap}). The project is at the reproduction gate. Its remaining items
are an independent audit, the soak test that localises the display-watchdog faults, and a
full-pipeline check that our trainer and scorer recover a known answer on a small model. Pilot
runs then fix four quantities that the protocol deliberately leaves to rules rather than to
judgement: the model scale, the length of Stage~B, the composition of the task-exposure stage and
the number of evaluation contexts per cell. The learning-rate sweep follows. Everything is then
frozen, including items, configurations, analysis code and the compiled-kernel cache, before the
first main run.

The core runs train the four anchor compositions with four to five seeds each. The controls and the
dose and budget branches follow, and then frozen evaluation, at which point the confirmatory
hypotheses are decided. The second tier adds $k=2$ and $k=6$ to resolve the curve, together with the
placement arms and the larger-state linear variant. Weeks are planned around the throughput measured
above: the essential tier fits in about four days of GPU time at D384, and the desirable tier adds
about a day and a half.

\paragraph{Risks and how they are handled.}
The most serious scientific risk is that the all-attention reference is itself poor at 32K tokens.
In that case no hybrid could be measured against it. The pilots test for this explicitly, and if the
reference fails at both 16K and 32K at both scales, the study pauses and is re-scoped either to
shorter contexts or to a larger model. A second risk is statistical power. Independent audits of the
frozen design estimate that seed-to-seed variation on below-ceiling recall may be about twice what
was planned for, which would make an ``inconclusive'' outcome for H1 considerably more likely than
the protocol assumed. A revision of the multiple-testing structure and of the seed budget is
therefore being decided before the freeze, at no GPU cost. The engineering risks are the watchdog
faults and the shared GPU, handled respectively by the soak test with automatic resumption and by
exclusive timing windows.

\paragraph{Scope.}
All conclusions will be stated for this package: about 28--49M parameters, Gated DeltaNet as the
linear layer, gated softmax attention as the full layer, this data and recipe, and capability
elicited by an identical synthetic exposure. Compute per token is not matched across compositions,
so the study cannot separate the value of unbounded memory from the value of extra computation.
Sliding-window attention, other linear mechanisms beyond an optional arm, and contexts longer than
32K are out of scope. Effects may differ at scale, as has been reported for multi-hop behaviour in
large hybrids, so we will treat agreement or disagreement with large-model reports as
corroboration, not proof.

\section{Concluding remarks}

The ratio of full to linear attention is one of the most consequential and least examined
choices in current long-context architectures. Its consequences for memory are easy to compute. Its
consequences for what a model can actually do with long context are not. This project measures
the second against the first in the most controlled setting we can afford. A family of models
identical except for their mixers is trained under one recipe and evaluated on tasks that separate
retrieval, composition and aggregation, at every length up to the training length, with costs
measured on the same implementation. The groundwork is in place: the question is sharpened by the
literature, the protocol has been independently reviewed and frozen, the instrument has been verified
against reference implementations, and the central phenomenon has been reproduced on our hardware.
What remains is to train the models and let the pre-registered tests decide.
